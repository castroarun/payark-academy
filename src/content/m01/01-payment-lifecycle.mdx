# How a Payment Flows: End to End

Every time you transfer money — whether tapping your phone to pay for coffee or wiring funds across the globe — a complex chain of events unfolds behind the scenes. Understanding this chain is the foundation of everything in payment systems engineering.

## What Is a Payment?

At its core, a **payment** is a transfer of value from one party (the **originator** or **payer**) to another (the **beneficiary** or **payee**). That sounds simple, but the infrastructure required to make it happen reliably, securely, and at scale is anything but.

A payment is not just "moving money." It is an **instruction** that travels through multiple systems, gets validated, transformed, cleared, and finally settled — with an audit trail every step of the way.

## The Five Stages

Every payment, whether domestic or cross-border, passes through five fundamental stages:

1. **Initiation**
2. **Validation**
3. **Clearing**
4. **Settlement**
5. **Reconciliation**

Let's walk through each one.

---

## Stage 1: Initiation

The payment lifecycle begins when the **originator instructs their bank** to make a payment. This instruction can come from a corporate treasury system, a mobile banking app, or even a paper form at a branch counter.

The bank creates a **payment message** — a structured data packet that describes the payment. In the world of cross-border payments, this message might be:

- **pain.001** — an ISO 20022 Payment Initiation message (customer to bank)
- **MT101** — a legacy SWIFT message for payment initiation

The message contains essential details: who is paying, who is receiving, how much, in what currency, and when the payment should be executed.

## Stage 2: Validation

Before the bank sends the payment anywhere, it runs a series of checks:

- **Funds availability** — Does the originator's account have sufficient balance?
- **Compliance screening** — Is the beneficiary on any sanctions lists (OFAC, EU, UN)? Does the transaction trigger any anti-money laundering (AML) alerts?
- **Format validation** — Is the payment message correctly structured? Are all mandatory fields present?
- **Business rules** — Does the payment comply with the bank's internal policies (e.g., transaction limits, approved currencies)?

If any check fails, the payment is rejected or held for manual review. This stage is critical because once a payment enters the clearing network, reversing it becomes difficult or impossible.

## Stage 3: Clearing

**Clearing** is the process of exchanging payment instructions between banks. It answers the question: *"Who owes what to whom?"*

The originator's bank sends the payment instruction to a **clearing network** — an intermediary system that collects instructions from multiple banks, validates them, and calculates the obligations between participants.

For domestic payments, this is typically a national clearing house:
- **FAST** (Singapore) — real-time retail payments
- **MEPS+** (Singapore) — large-value RTGS
- **CHAPS** (UK) — same-day sterling payments
- **UPI** (India) — unified mobile payments
- **NEFT** (India) — batch electronic transfers

For cross-border payments, the instructions travel through the **SWIFT network** and may pass through one or more **correspondent banks**.

It is important to understand that clearing is about **instructions**, not money. No actual funds move during clearing — that happens next.

## Stage 4: Settlement

**Settlement** is the actual movement of funds between bank accounts. This is where money truly changes hands.

In most systems, settlement happens at the **central bank**. Each participating bank holds a settlement account at the central bank. When a payment is settled:

1. The central bank **debits** the sending bank's settlement account
2. The central bank **credits** the receiving bank's settlement account

This transfer is final, irrevocable, and guaranteed by the central bank — a concept known as **settlement finality**.

There are two main approaches to settlement:

- **RTGS (Real-Time Gross Settlement)** — Each payment is settled individually, in real-time. Used for large-value payments.
- **DNS (Deferred Net Settlement)** — Payments are accumulated over a period, net positions are calculated, and only the net amounts are settled. Used for high-volume, lower-value payments.

## Stage 5: Reconciliation

After settlement, both the sending and receiving banks **verify their records**. Reconciliation ensures that:

- Every payment instruction sent was processed correctly
- The amounts debited and credited match
- No payments were lost, duplicated, or altered
- The bank's internal ledger matches the central bank's records

Reconciliation typically happens at **end of day (EOD)** but can also run intraday for critical systems. Discrepancies trigger investigation workflows.

---

## Domestic vs Cross-Border

**Domestic payments** are relatively straightforward: the payment flows through a single national clearing network and settles at one central bank. The originator and beneficiary are in the same country, using the same currency.

**Cross-border payments** are more complex. They involve:

- Multiple currencies (requiring FX conversion)
- Multiple clearing and settlement systems
- **Correspondent banking** — intermediary banks that bridge the gap between banks in different countries
- The **SWIFT network** for messaging between banks
- Potentially different regulatory regimes and compliance requirements

A cross-border payment chain might look like:

**Originator** → **Originator Bank** → **Correspondent Bank A** → **Correspondent Bank B** → **Beneficiary Bank** → **Beneficiary**

Each hop adds time, cost, and complexity.

## A Real-World Example

Let's trace what happens when you send **SGD 100 from DBS to OCBC** using FAST in Singapore:

1. **Initiation** — You open the DBS app and enter the transfer details. DBS creates a payment message.
2. **Validation** — DBS checks your balance, screens the transaction, and validates the message format.
3. **Clearing** — DBS sends the instruction to FAST (operated by the Banking Computer Services division). FAST validates and forwards it to OCBC.
4. **Settlement** — The funds are settled through MEPS+ (Singapore's RTGS). MAS debits DBS's settlement account and credits OCBC's.
5. **Reconciliation** — Both DBS and OCBC verify the transaction against their internal records at EOD.

The entire process takes **seconds** for the end user, but involves multiple systems, checks, and settlement mechanisms working in concert.

---

## Key Terms

- **Originator (Payer)** — The party sending the payment
- **Beneficiary (Payee)** — The party receiving the payment
- **Intermediary** — Any bank or system between the originator and beneficiary
- **Debit** — A reduction in an account balance (money going out)
- **Credit** — An increase in an account balance (money coming in)
- **Value Date** — The date when funds become available to the beneficiary
- **Settlement Finality** — The point at which a payment is irrevocable and unconditional

> **Key Takeaway:** A payment is not a single event — it is a lifecycle. Understanding the five stages (Initiation, Validation, Clearing, Settlement, Reconciliation) is essential because every component in a payment system maps to one or more of these stages. When you build payment software, you are building infrastructure that participates in this lifecycle.
