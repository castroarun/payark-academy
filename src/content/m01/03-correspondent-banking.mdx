# Correspondent Banking: Nostro, Vostro, and SWIFT

Cross-border payments are fundamentally more complex than domestic ones. When you send money to someone in another country, your bank probably does not have a direct connection to the recipient's bank. So how does the money get there? The answer is **correspondent banking** — a network of relationships between banks that enables money to flow across borders.

## Why Correspondent Banking Exists

Not every bank can maintain direct relationships with every other bank in the world. There are roughly 25,000 financial institutions across 200+ countries. Establishing bilateral agreements, technical connections, and regulatory compliance with each one is impractical.

Instead, banks establish relationships with a smaller number of **correspondent banks** — typically large, internationally active banks that have broad networks of their own. These correspondents act as intermediaries, providing services on behalf of banks that do not have a direct presence in a particular country or currency.

Think of it like air travel: not every city has direct flights to every other city. Instead, you fly through hub airports. Correspondent banks are the hubs of international finance.

## What Is a Correspondent Bank?

A **correspondent bank** is a financial institution that provides services on behalf of another bank (the **respondent bank**). These services include:

- **Payment processing** — Receiving and forwarding payment instructions
- **Account services** — Holding accounts (nostro/vostro) for the respondent bank
- **Foreign exchange** — Converting currencies as part of cross-border payments
- **Cash management** — Managing liquidity positions for the respondent

The correspondent bank acts as an agent, executing transactions in its local market that the respondent bank cannot do directly.

## Nostro and Vostro Accounts

At the heart of correspondent banking are two types of accounts that are actually the **same account viewed from different perspectives**:

### Nostro Account — "Our Account at Your Bank"

The word *nostro* comes from Latin, meaning "ours." A **nostro account** is an account that a bank holds **at another bank**, typically in the other bank's local currency.

**Example:** DBS (Singapore) holds a GBP account at HSBC (UK). From DBS's perspective, this is their **nostro account** — their money, held at HSBC, denominated in British pounds.

DBS uses this nostro account to make and receive GBP payments. When DBS needs to send GBP to a beneficiary in the UK, it instructs HSBC to debit its nostro account and pay the beneficiary.

### Vostro Account — "Your Account at Our Bank"

The word *vostro* means "yours." A **vostro account** is the mirror image of a nostro account — it is the same account, but viewed from the correspondent bank's perspective.

**Example:** The same GBP account that DBS calls their "nostro at HSBC" is called a "vostro account for DBS" from HSBC's perspective. HSBC holds this account on its books as a liability — it is DBS's money that HSBC is holding.

### The Mirror Relationship

| Perspective | What They Call It | Meaning |
|-------------|-------------------|---------|
| DBS (account holder) | Nostro account | "Our account at your bank" |
| HSBC (account provider) | Vostro account | "Your account at our bank" |

This mirror relationship is fundamental. Every nostro account at one bank is simultaneously a vostro account at the other. When you hear "nostro/vostro," remember: same account, two viewpoints.

## How a Cross-Border Payment Works

Let's trace a real example. Say a company in Singapore (originator) wants to pay a supplier in London (beneficiary) GBP 50,000.

**Actors:**
- **Originator** — Singapore company
- **DBS** — Originator's bank (Singapore)
- **HSBC** — DBS's correspondent in the UK (holds DBS's nostro account)
- **Barclays** — Beneficiary's bank (UK)
- **Beneficiary** — London supplier

**Payment flow:**

1. The originator instructs DBS to pay GBP 50,000 to the beneficiary's account at Barclays
2. DBS creates a SWIFT MT103 message with the payment details
3. DBS sends the MT103 to HSBC (its UK correspondent)
4. HSBC receives the instruction and **debits DBS's nostro account** by GBP 50,000
5. HSBC sends the GBP 50,000 to Barclays through the UK domestic clearing system (CHAPS or FPS)
6. Barclays **credits the beneficiary's account** with GBP 50,000
7. The beneficiary receives the funds

In this flow, HSBC is the bridge between the Singapore banking system and the UK banking system. DBS does not need a direct connection to Barclays — HSBC handles the local leg.

## The SWIFT Network

**SWIFT** — the Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication — is the backbone of international banking communication. Founded in 1973 and headquartered in Belgium, SWIFT connects over 11,000 financial institutions in 200+ countries.

### What SWIFT Is (and Is Not)

This is a critical distinction: **SWIFT is a messaging network, not a payment system.** SWIFT does not hold accounts, does not settle payments, and does not move money. It carries the **instructions** that tell banks what to do.

Think of SWIFT as the postal service for banks. It delivers messages reliably and securely, but the actual transfer of funds happens through clearing and settlement systems.

### SWIFT Codes (BIC)

Every institution on the SWIFT network is identified by a **BIC** (Bank Identifier Code), also known as a SWIFT code. A BIC is either **8 or 11 characters** long:

| Position | Meaning | Example |
|----------|---------|---------|
| 1-4 | Bank code | `DBSS` (DBS) |
| 5-6 | Country code | `SG` (Singapore) |
| 7-8 | Location code | `SG` |
| 9-11 | Branch code (optional) | `XXX` or specific branch |

**Example:** `DBSSSGSG` is DBS Bank in Singapore. `HSBCGB2L` is HSBC in the UK.

The 8-character code identifies the head office. The 11-character code identifies a specific branch.

### Key SWIFT Message Types

SWIFT uses a numbering system for its message types. The most important for payments:

- **MT103** — Single Customer Credit Transfer. This is the workhorse of cross-border payments. It carries the full details of a customer payment: originator, beneficiary, amount, currency, charges, and payment purpose.

- **MT202** — General Financial Institution Transfer. Used for bank-to-bank movements that are not directly related to a customer payment (e.g., funding a nostro account).

- **MT202COV** — Cover Payment. A variant of MT202 specifically used in cover payment methods, where the bank-to-bank settlement travels separately from the customer instruction.

- **MT199** — Free Format Message. Used for general correspondence between banks — payment investigations, queries, amendment requests, and ad-hoc communications.

### Migration to ISO 20022 (MX Messages)

SWIFT is in the process of migrating from legacy MT messages to **ISO 20022 MX messages**. The new format uses **XML** (structured markup language), providing significantly richer data than the fixed-format MT messages.

Key replacements:

| Legacy MT | ISO 20022 MX | Purpose |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| MT103 | **pacs.008** | Customer credit transfer |
| MT202 | **pacs.009** | Financial institution transfer |
| MT900/MT910 | **camt.054** | Debit/credit notifications |
| MT940/MT950 | **camt.053** | Statement of account |

The migration to ISO 20022 is not just a technical format change — it enables **richer data** for compliance screening, **better straight-through processing**, and **improved interoperability** between payment systems worldwide.

## Payment Chains: Serial vs Cover

When a payment travels through multiple correspondent banks, there are two main methods for routing:

### Serial Method

In the **serial method**, the payment message (MT103) passes through **each bank in the chain sequentially**:

**Originator Bank** → **Correspondent 1** → **Correspondent 2** → **Beneficiary Bank**

Each bank in the chain receives the full MT103, processes it, and forwards it to the next bank. Settlement happens at each hop — Correspondent 1 debits the originator bank's nostro, then instructs Correspondent 2, and so on.

**Advantages:** Simple, each bank in the chain sees the full payment details
**Disadvantages:** Slower (each bank processes sequentially), each intermediary can deduct fees

### Cover Method

In the **cover method**, the payment instruction and the settlement travel on **separate paths**:

- The **MT103** (customer instruction) is sent directly from the originator bank to the beneficiary bank
- The **MT202COV** (cover payment for settlement) is sent through the correspondent chain

This means the beneficiary bank receives the payment details immediately, even though the actual settlement is still working its way through the correspondent chain.

**Advantages:** Faster notification to beneficiary bank, beneficiary bank has full details upfront
**Disadvantages:** More complex, requires coordination between two message paths

## Liquidity Management

Banks must carefully manage the **liquidity** in their nostro accounts. Having too much money sitting in a nostro account means that capital is idle and not earning a return. Having too little means payments will fail or be delayed.

Key liquidity management activities include:

- **Forecasting** — Predicting expected payment volumes and directions to plan funding
- **Pre-funding** — Transferring funds to nostro accounts before the business day starts
- **Intraday monitoring** — Tracking nostro balances throughout the day and topping up as needed
- **End-of-day sweeping** — Moving excess funds from nostro accounts back to the head office

Poor liquidity management is a leading cause of cross-border payment delays. When a bank's nostro runs dry, all outgoing payments in that currency queue up until the account is funded.

## Key Terms

- **BIC (Bank Identifier Code)** — The 8 or 11 character code identifying a financial institution on the SWIFT network
- **SWIFT** — Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication; the global messaging network for banks
- **Correspondent bank** — A bank that provides services on behalf of another bank (the respondent)
- **Respondent bank** — A bank that uses a correspondent bank's services
- **Intermediary bank** — Any bank in the payment chain between the originator and beneficiary banks
- **Cover method** — Payment method where customer instruction (MT103) and settlement (MT202COV) travel on separate paths
- **Serial method** — Payment method where the MT103 passes through each bank in the chain sequentially
- **Payment chain** — The full sequence of banks involved in routing a payment from originator to beneficiary

> **Key Takeaway:** Correspondent banking is the plumbing of international finance. It works through a network of nostro/vostro account relationships that enable banks to send and receive payments in currencies and countries where they do not have a direct presence. SWIFT provides the messaging backbone but does not move money — actual settlement depends on clearing and settlement systems in each country. Understanding this architecture is essential because every cross-border payment system you build must account for correspondent chains, nostro funding, message routing, and the distinction between serial and cover payment methods.
