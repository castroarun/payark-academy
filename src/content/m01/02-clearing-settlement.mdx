# Clearing vs Settlement: RTGS and DNS

One of the most fundamental distinctions in payment systems is the difference between **clearing** and **settlement**. These terms are often used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they represent two very different processes — and understanding the distinction is critical for anyone building payment infrastructure.

## Clearing: The Exchange of Instructions

**Clearing** is the process of exchanging payment instructions between participating banks and calculating what each bank owes to — or is owed by — the others.

Think of it as the "accounting" step. During clearing:

- Banks submit payment instructions to a **clearing house** (or clearing system)
- The clearing house **validates** each instruction
- The clearing house **calculates obligations** — who owes how much to whom
- The clearing house may perform **netting** — reducing the total number of settlement transfers needed

No actual money moves during clearing. It is purely about information: collecting, validating, and organizing payment instructions so that settlement can happen efficiently.

## Settlement: The Actual Movement of Money

**Settlement** is when funds actually move between bank accounts — typically at the central bank. This is where the financial obligations calculated during clearing are fulfilled.

After clearing determines that Bank A owes Bank B a certain amount, settlement is the process of debiting Bank A's central bank account and crediting Bank B's central bank account.

Settlement is the moment of truth: once it happens, the transfer is **final and irrevocable**.

## Why Are They Separate?

Separating clearing from settlement serves a critical purpose: **efficiency through netting**.

Consider a scenario: During one day, Bank A sends 1,000 payments to Bank B totaling $50 million, and Bank B sends 800 payments to Bank A totaling $45 million. Without netting, the central bank would need to process 1,800 individual transfers.

With **netting**, the clearing house calculates that Bank A's net obligation to Bank B is only $5 million. Instead of 1,800 transfers, a single $5 million settlement handles everything. This dramatically reduces:

- The volume of central bank transactions
- The liquidity each bank needs to hold
- The operational load on settlement systems

## RTGS: Real-Time Gross Settlement

In an **RTGS** system, each payment is settled **individually** and **in real-time**. There is no batching, no netting, and no waiting. The moment a valid payment instruction arrives, the central bank debits the sender and credits the receiver.

### How RTGS Works

1. Bank A submits a payment instruction to the RTGS system
2. The system verifies Bank A has sufficient funds in its settlement account
3. If funds are available, the system **immediately** debits Bank A and credits Bank B
4. The payment achieves **settlement finality** — it is done, irrevocable, and guaranteed

### Examples of RTGS Systems

| System | Country | Operator |
|--------|---------|----------|
| **MEPS+** | Singapore | Monetary Authority of Singapore |
| **CHAPS** | United Kingdom | Bank of England |
| **RTGS** | India | Reserve Bank of India |
| **Fedwire** | United States | Federal Reserve |
| **TARGET2** | Eurozone | European Central Bank |

### Advantages of RTGS

- **No counterparty risk** — Settlement is immediate, so there is no risk of a bank defaulting before settlement occurs
- **Immediate finality** — The payment is complete the moment it settles
- **High certainty** — Both banks know the exact status of every payment in real-time

### Disadvantages of RTGS

- **High liquidity requirements** — Banks must maintain large balances at the central bank to cover each individual payment as it comes in
- **Expensive per transaction** — The infrastructure cost and liquidity cost make RTGS impractical for small-value retail payments
- **Throughput limitations** — Processing each payment individually is resource-intensive

RTGS is typically used for **large-value, time-critical payments** — interbank transfers, securities settlements, and high-value corporate payments.

## DNS: Deferred Net Settlement

In a **DNS** system, payments are not settled immediately. Instead, they are **accumulated** over a defined period (a **clearing cycle**), and the clearing house calculates the **net position** of each participant. Only the net amounts are then settled.

### How DNS Works

1. During the clearing cycle (e.g., every 2 hours, or once at end of day), banks submit payment instructions to the clearing house
2. The clearing house collects and validates all instructions
3. At the end of the cycle, the clearing house performs **multilateral netting** — calculating each bank's net obligation
4. Only the net amounts are settled at the central bank
5. Banks receive reports showing which payments were processed

### Examples of DNS Systems

| System | Country | Cycle |
|--------|---------|-------|
| **NEFT** | India | Half-hourly batches |
| **Legacy ACH** | United States | Same-day or next-day batches |
| **BACS** | United Kingdom | 3-day cycle |

### Netting in Practice

Imagine three banks at the end of a clearing cycle:

- Bank A owes Bank B $10M and Bank C $5M
- Bank B owes Bank A $8M and Bank C $3M
- Bank C owes Bank A $4M and Bank B $6M

**Without netting:** 6 separate transfers totaling $36M

**With multilateral netting:** The clearing house calculates:
- Bank A's net: owes $3M ($15M out, $12M in)
- Bank B's net: receives $1M ($11M in, $10M out)
- Bank C's net: owes $2M ($9M in, $11M out... wait, let us recalculate)

The exact numbers depend on the netting algorithm, but the principle is clear: the total settlement volume is dramatically reduced compared to gross settlement.

### Advantages of DNS

- **Lower liquidity requirements** — Banks only need to cover their net position, not the gross total of all payments
- **Higher efficiency** — Netting reduces the number of central bank transactions significantly
- **Lower per-transaction cost** — Ideal for high-volume, lower-value payments

### Disadvantages of DNS

- **Settlement risk** — During the accumulation period, a bank could fail before settlement occurs, leaving other banks exposed
- **Delayed finality** — Payments are not truly "settled" until the end of the clearing cycle
- **Operational complexity** — Managing clearing cycles, cutoff times, and failed settlements adds complexity

## Real-Time Retail Payments: The Hybrid Approach

Modern real-time retail payment systems — **FAST** (Singapore), **FPS** (UK), **UPI** (India) — provide **instant payment confirmation** to end users. But under the hood, the settlement model varies:

- Some use **real-time settlement** (effectively RTGS) for each payment
- Some use **deferred settlement** — the payment notification is instant, but actual settlement happens later in batches
- Some use a **pre-funded model** — banks pre-fund a pool at the central bank, and payments are settled against this pool instantly

From the customer's perspective, the payment is instant. But the mechanics of how the banks actually settle with each other can be either gross or net, real-time or deferred.

This is an important architectural distinction: **customer experience** (instant notification) is separate from **interbank settlement** (how banks square up).

## How Clearing Houses Work

A clearing house sits between banks and performs several critical functions:

1. **Receive** payment instructions from all participating banks
2. **Validate** each instruction (format, limits, participant status)
3. **Calculate** obligations using bilateral or multilateral netting
4. **Instruct** the settlement system to transfer the net amounts
5. **Report** to each bank the details of all payments processed
6. **Handle exceptions** — rejected payments, formatting errors, insufficient funds

Clearing houses are operated by central banks, banking associations, or specialized financial market infrastructure companies. They are heavily regulated and subject to strict operational standards.

## Operating Hours and Cutoffs

Most payment systems have defined **operating hours**:

- **RTGS systems** typically operate during business hours (e.g., MEPS+ operates 9:00 AM to 7:15 PM Singapore time) with some systems extending hours for cross-border settlements
- **DNS systems** process in defined cycles with **cutoff times** — payments submitted after the cutoff are included in the next cycle
- **Real-time retail systems** (FAST, FPS, UPI) generally operate **24/7/365**, though the underlying settlement may still follow business-hour schedules

Cutoff times are critical for payment operations. Missing a cutoff can mean a payment is delayed by hours or an entire business day. Payment systems engineers must design their systems to handle cutoffs gracefully — queuing payments, alerting operations teams, and managing customer expectations.

## Key Terms

- **Finality** — The legally defined point at which a payment transfer is irrevocable
- **Irrevocability** — Once settled, the payment cannot be reversed
- **Netting** — Calculating net obligations to reduce the number of settlement transfers
- **Multilateral netting** — Netting across all participants simultaneously
- **Bilateral netting** — Netting between two specific participants
- **Clearing cycle** — The defined period during which payments are accumulated before netting and settlement
- **Gross settlement** — Settling each payment individually (no netting)
- **Net settlement** — Settling only the net amounts after netting

> **Key Takeaway:** Clearing and settlement are fundamentally different processes. Clearing is about exchanging and organizing information; settlement is about moving actual money. RTGS eliminates counterparty risk but requires high liquidity. DNS is efficient but introduces settlement risk. Modern payment systems often combine elements of both to balance speed, cost, risk, and liquidity.
